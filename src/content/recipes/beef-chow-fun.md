## Chef's Note

Beef chow fun is a Cantonese dish built around wide rice noodles, beef and a thin soy coating. This version follows the dry style, with no thick gravy. Flexible noodles, enough oil and small portions make the technique workable on a home stove. A few toasted surfaces are welcome; restaurant wok hei is not a promise that every domestic burner can fulfill, and burned oil is not the goal.

## Directions

1. **Coat the beef:** Stir the beef's tablespoon each of soy and wine, its two tablespoons water, cornstarch and measured baking soda until dispersed. Add the thinly sliced beef, turn and gently mix until evenly coated, then mix in its teaspoon of oil. Cover and refrigerate for twenty minutes while preparing everything else. Do not rinse off this small-dose coating or add more baking soda.
2. **Prepare flexible noodles:** Separate supple fresh noodles gently. If they are cold and stiff, follow the package warming directions. Where the package permits microwaving, transfer noodles out of non-microwave-safe packaging to a microwave-safe plate, cover loosely and warm in short 20–30-second intervals, checking and turning between intervals until they separate without force. Work in manageable portions; one fixed thirty-second blast is not enough for every cold block. Cut whole sheets into 3/4–1-inch ribbons if necessary. Divide the prepared noodles into four 170 g portions.
3. **Mix and portion:** Whisk the noodle soy, Chinese dark soy, noodle wine, sugar and sesame oil, then divide the mixture into four equal portions. Prepare ginger, scallions and drained sprouts. Keep scallion whites and greens separate; divide each and the ginger into four portions. Set out clean bowls for the cooked sprouts and beef.
4. **Cook sprouts separately:** Heat 1 tsp of the cooking oil in the pan over medium-high heat. Add the sprouts and stir for about one minute. Add their tablespoon of water, cover for 1–2 minutes, then uncover and let free water evaporate. The sprouts should be steaming hot and softened through their stems, not merely warmed on the surface; extend briefly if needed. Transfer to a clean bowl and divide into four portions.
5. **Cook two beef batches:** Let the pan dry and recover heat. Add 1 1/2 tsp cooking oil and spread half the beef in one layer, leaving excess loose coating in its raw-meat bowl. Cook about one minute until browned below, turn, and cook another 1–3 minutes as needed. Check representative thicker slices with a fine probe inserted sideways from the edge: at least 145°F / 63°C. Transfer to a clean bowl. Repeat with another 1 1/2 tsp oil and the other half. Discard leftover raw-contact coating and wash its bowl and tools. Divide the cooked beef and its juices into four portions; each has time to rest at least three minutes before serving.
6. **Finish one noodle portion:** Reduce heat if the pan is smoking heavily; remove any blackened deposits. Add 2 tsp of the remaining cooking oil over medium-high heat. Stir in one portion of ginger and scallion whites for about 20 seconds until fragrant. Add one noodle portion and lift gently to coat, then let it touch the pan for 15–20 seconds at a time, lifting and turning between contacts. Add one portion of noodle seasoning and fold until evenly coated. Add one portion each of cooked beef, juices, sprouts and scallion greens. Fold just until hot throughout, about 3–4 minutes for this whole finishing step. If firm noodles need moisture, use a little of the reserved water rather than adding extra soy; the full recipe has two tablespoons reserved across all four portions.
7. **Plate and repeat:** Serve the first portion promptly, confirming at least three minutes have passed since its beef reached 145°F / 63°C. Repeat step 6 three more times, letting the empty pan recover heat between portions before adding each fresh 2 tsp oil; add aromatics once that oil shimmers. Use the remaining measured ingredient portions. Serve as four moderate noodle-main portions. A separately prepared vegetable side can accompany them; extra rice is unnecessary because the noodles already supply the starch.
