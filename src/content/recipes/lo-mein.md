## Chef's Note

Lo mein means "scooped noodles" — 撈 (_lo_) is the motion of digging under the pile and turning it over, and the noodles are boiled first and folded into the wok at the very end. That's what separates it from chow mein, where the noodles do their cooking in the pan. Two habits carry the dish at home: give the protein a short cornstarch coat so it stays tender under high heat, and cook it separately from everything else. A protein that needs 90 seconds and noodles that need several minutes can't share a pan.

## Directions

1.  **Prep the protein (15 min, hands-off).**
    - **Shrimp:** Toss with the kosher salt and baking soda and refrigerate 15 to 30 minutes. The salt firms the proteins, the baking soda raises the surface pH, and together they give you that snappy restaurant bite. Don't go past 30 minutes — longer and they turn oddly hard and taste faintly soapy. Skip the soy-and-garlic marinade you see in a lot of recipes: shrimp cook in 90 seconds and won't absorb it, minced garlic scorches at wok heat, and all you get is wet shrimp that steam instead of sear.
    - **Chicken:** No brine. Toss the sliced thighs with the cornstarch, oil, salt, and white pepper and let stand 15 minutes — no longer. Coat it hours ahead and the salt and starch cure it into something rubbery. Skip ahead to step 2.
2.  **Whisk the sauce and put it by the stove:** Combine both soy sauces, oyster sauce, stock, sugar, and white pepper. Once the wok is hot you'll have about eight seconds between steps — there's no measuring after that point. Two notes on what's in it. The dark soy is doing color work, not salt work; without it the noodles stay pale and this doesn't read as lo mein. And **the sesame oil is deliberately not here** — its aroma compounds boil off around 275-355°F and a hot wok runs well past that, so sesame oil added now contributes nothing a neutral oil wouldn't. It goes in off the heat at the end.
3.  **Set the station — and dry the bok choy:** Split the bok choy: stems sliced on a bias into 1/2-inch pieces, leaves left whole or halved. Wash them separately (grit hides at the base of the stems) and **spin or towel them properly dry**. Wet bok choy dumps water into the wok and you end up braising the noodles instead of glazing them. Shiitakes in one bowl, bok choy stems in a second, leaves in a third, garlic-ginger-scallion whites in a fourth, sprouts and scallion greens in a fifth. Line them up left to right in cooking order. This is where stir-fry is actually won — the cooking itself is eight minutes of chaos, and prep is what buys you the calm before it.
4.  **Noodles, deliberately underdone — then let them warm back up:** Boil the noodles 45 to 60 seconds shy of package time; they'll finish in the wok. Drain and rinse under cold water for about 30 seconds — this stops the cooking _and_ strips the surface starch that makes them gluey. Shake the colander until they stop dripping, then toss with 1 tbsp of the scallion-shallot oil **and 1 tbsp of the sauce**. The oil keeps them from fusing into a brick; the spoonful of sauce seasons them from the inside, which plain oil can't do.

    **Then leave them on the counter, not in the fridge.** Cold noodles hitting the wok tank the pan temperature, and on a home burner you have no heat to spare. They want to be at room temperature when they go in. Cook them within 30 minutes, though — sit longer than that and they dry out.

5.  **Finish the shrimp prep (skip if using chicken):** Rinse the brine off under cold water — leftover baking soda tastes soapy — then press hard between paper towels. If you have ten minutes, spread them on a paper-towel-lined plate uncovered in the fridge. Surface moisture is the single biggest lever between sear and steam. Toss with the cornstarch and 1 tsp neutral oil.
6.  **Sear the protein in two batches:** Heat the dry wok over high heat until it just begins to smoke, then add 1 tbsp of the scallion-shallot oil.
    - **Shrimp:** Half the shrimp in a single layer, untouched for 45 seconds. Flip, 20 to 30 seconds more, and pull them at about **80 percent** — still slightly translucent at the thickest part. They finish on residual heat at the end, and shrimp that look done now will be rubber by the time you eat.
    - **Chicken:** Half the chicken in a single layer, untouched for 90 seconds to build color, then toss 45 seconds more until just cooked through.
    - Either way, two batches. One crowded batch steams instead of searing — this is the step people skip and then wonder about.
7.  **Brown the shiitakes before anything else touches the wok:** Last 1 tbsp of oil into the hot wok, then the mushrooms spread in as close to a single layer as you can manage. **Leave them alone for 2 minutes.** They'll release water first and look wrong — wait it out. Once that water cooks off they start browning, and that's where the depth comes from. Toss for 1 minute more. Mushrooms drink oil; if the wok goes dry and the pieces start to scorch, add another splash rather than letting them stick. Rushing this step is how shiitakes end up gray and slippery instead of meaty.
8.  **Aromatics, 20 seconds:** Push the mushrooms up the side of the wok, add the garlic, ginger, and scallion whites to the center, and stir constantly until fragrant but not colored. Splash the Shaoxing wine around the rim so it hits hot metal and flashes off aromatic rather than pooling raw in the bottom.
9.  **Bok choy, stems then leaves:** Stems first, 90 seconds, tossing constantly — they should stay squeaky and just start to go translucent at the edges. Leaves next, 30 seconds, until barely collapsed. Stop early; they keep cooking with the noodles and again on the plate, and overcooked bok choy leaves turn to slime.
10. **Noodles and sauce — scoop, don't toss:** Add the noodles, then pour the remaining sauce **around the rim of the wok** rather than over the top. It sears against the hot metal on the way down and picks up a smoky, caramelized soy note; poured into the middle it just steams. Now work the noodles for 1 to 2 minutes with a scooping motion — dig the spatula under the pile, lift, turn it over, and scrape the bottom of the wok each pass. This is the motion the dish is named for: _lo_ (撈) means to scoop or dredge up. A tossing motion skates over the top and leaves the strands at the bottom to scorch and the ones on top pale. Keep going until every strand is evenly bronze and nothing pools in the bottom. A splash of hot water — hot, not cold — loosens things if the noodles clump.
11. **Finish off the heat:** Kill the burner. Return the protein with the bean sprouts and scallion greens and toss 30 seconds in the residual heat — the sprouts should stay raw-crisp, they're the only pure crunch in the bowl. Now the sesame oil, off the flame, so it keeps its aroma instead of burning it off. **If you're using the vinegar, it goes in here too** — a teaspoon won't read as sour, it just lifts the soy off the palate. It's the one thing in this recipe that isn't in the version most people cook; try the dish without it first, then add it to your own bowl and see whether you want it in the wok next time. Scatter crisp shallot-scallion bits over each bowl and serve immediately.

## Scaling

**Everything here is calibrated to 16 oz of noodles.** That's one standard package of fresh lo mein or Hong Kong egg noodles, and it's the number to scale from — not servings, not protein weight.

Per 16 oz of noodles:

| Component         | Amount                                                                                                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Protein           | 16 oz                                                                                                  |
| Sauce             | 3 tbsp light soy + 1 tbsp dark soy + 1 tbsp oyster + 2 tbsp stock + 1 tsp sugar + 1/8 tsp white pepper |
| Cooking fat       | 3 tbsp, divided (1 noodles / 1 protein / 1 mushrooms)                                                  |
| Aromatics         | 3 cloves garlic + 1 tbsp ginger + 4 scallions                                                          |
| Cooked vegetables | 20 oz raw total (8 oz shiitake + 12 oz bok choy)                                                       |
| Raw finish        | 2 cups bean sprouts                                                                                    |
| Off the heat      | 2 tsp sesame oil + 1 tsp black vinegar (optional)                                                      |

**Doubling to 32 oz:** double everything, but cook it in two complete rounds rather than one giant one. A double batch will not fit in a 14-inch wok, and forcing it there is how you end up with steamed noodles.

**If you load in more vegetables than the table says,** add roughly 1 tbsp light soy and 1 tsp oyster sauce per extra 8 oz. Shiitakes in particular are sponges — this is the most likely reason a batch tastes flat despite correct measurements.

## Two Schools of Lo Mein

The method above is the fast-toss version: high heat, sauce reduced onto the noodles in about 90 seconds, nothing thickened. It's what most Cantonese home cooks and Grace Young teach, and it's the one that rewards a hot wok.

There's a second, equally legitimate approach from restaurant kitchens — Chef Lau of Made With Lau, who has made this dish for decades, does it this way. Add the noodles to the seasoned vegetables, **cover the wok, and let them stew in the sauce for 2 to 3 minutes**, then finish with a cornstarch slurry poured in slowly at the very end. The noodles absorb sauce internally rather than just wearing it, and the slurry gives you a glossier coat. His reason for adding the slurry late is worth knowing: thicken early and the noodles can't absorb anything.

Pick by your stove. **If you have a strong burner or an outdoor wok ring, use the fast toss** — you'll get better texture and some char. **If you're on a weak electric or induction hob** where the wok never really recovers heat, the covered-stew version is more forgiving and will taste better than a sad, lukewarm toss. What you should not do is stew it _and_ expect wok hei.

## Variations

- **Pork:** 16 oz pork shoulder or loin, sliced thin and velveted like the chicken. Sear 90 seconds per batch.
- **Tofu:** 16 oz extra-firm, pressed 20 minutes and cubed. Skip the cornstarch toss and pan-fry in a nonstick skillet until browned on two sides; it won't survive wok tossing otherwise.
- **Edamame:** A cup of shelled edamame, added with the bok choy leaves, is the easiest way to make this land with kids — sweet, familiar, and it bumps the protein. It counts toward the 20 oz vegetable budget, so drop the bok choy to 8 oz or add the extra soy and oyster from the scaling note.
- **Heat:** A spoonful of [homemade chili oil](homemade-chili-oil) stirred in at the table, not in the wok, so the kids' bowls stay mild.
- **Dried shiitakes instead of fresh:** Soak 2 oz dried shiitakes in 1 1/2 cups hot water for 30 minutes, then slice. Dried mushrooms roughly quadruple in weight rehydrating, so 2 oz dried is what stands in for the 8 oz fresh. Strain the soaking liquid through a coffee filter and use 2 tbsp of it in place of the stock — it's the single cheapest upgrade to the sauce, and the reason to keep a bag of dried shiitakes in the pantry. Squeeze the caps dry before they hit the wok.
- **Carrots:** Left out on purpose. The bok choy stems already give you the hard crunch, the shiitakes give the depth, and raw-sweet carrot fights the sugar that's already in the sauce. If you want the color — or you're feeding a kid who eats by sight — one julienned carrot with the bok choy stems does no harm.
- **Other vegetable swaps:** Snow peas (with the bok choy leaves), broccoli florets, or napa cabbage all work. Keep the cooked-vegetable total at 20 oz raw, per the scaling table, and add them in order of density.

## Make It Work on a Tuesday

- **The brine and the velvet are the only things you can't rush**, and both are passive. Start the protein the moment you walk in the door; everything else fits inside that 15-minute window.
- **Sunday prep:** make a batch of [scallion-shallot oil](scallion-shallot-oil) and whisk the sauce — both keep the full week and cut this to a 20-minute cook. Slice the shiitakes no more than two days ahead; cut mushrooms darken and go slippery past that. Wash and cut the bok choy the day of, though — cut bok choy goes limp fast, and wet bok choy is the one thing that can ruin the wok stage.
- **Kid factor:** the noodles and bok choy stems are the safe core; mushrooms are the likeliest holdout. Slice the shiitakes thin enough to disappear into the noodles, or pull a portion before the mushrooms go in. Same trick for the protein — set aside a bowl of noodles and greens before it comes back to the wok.
- **No wok?** A 12-inch carbon steel or cast iron skillet works. You'll lose some char, so cook the vegetables in two batches rather than crowding.
- **If it still doesn't taste like the restaurant, it's the burner, not you.** A home range tops out around 12,000 BTU; a commercial wok burner runs 65,000 or more. You can't out-technique that gap, but you can stop fighting it: **cook the whole dish in two rounds instead of one.** Half the protein, half the vegetables, half the noodles, plated and held while you run the second round — it takes six extra minutes and it is the single biggest difference-maker available without new equipment. A full load for five people on a 12,000 BTU burner steams no matter how good your prep is.
- **Two smaller things that cost nothing:** use the most powerful burner on the range (usually front right), and preheat the empty wok a full minute longer than feels necessary. Recovery time between additions is what you're short on.
- **Leftovers:** reheat in a hot dry pan with a splash of water — the microwave turns the noodles gummy and the shrimp to rubber.
