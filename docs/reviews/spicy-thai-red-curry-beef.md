# Thai Red Curry Beef: individual editorial review

Date: 2026-09-06. Editorial review only; no physical kitchen test, measured yield or meal certification.

Challenged the complete saved proposal against the current recipe, saved original HTML/text and relevant follow-history changes. The export explicitly credits Myrecipes.com and the title “Spicy Thai Red Curry Beef”; the original URL is http://www.myrecipes.com/recipe/spicy-thai-red-curry-beef. Retain that recovered attribution as “Adapted from MyRecipes”; no individual author is evidenced. The live original was inaccessible in the prior source review. The HTML has the same light-coconut-milk/cream-cap contradiction, so it is not a reason to retain the flawed technique.

Preserve current 12 ounces tenderloin (flank remains a described alternative), the 13.5-ounce can of light coconut milk, 4 teaspoons red paste, 2 teaspoons each brown sugar and fish sauce, 1 1/2 cups sliced pepper, 1 cup sliced onion, the full cup basil, 1 tablespoon lime juice and 4 serving lime wedges. Reject the earlier proposal's invented 180 g/150 g vegetable equivalents, automatically specified Thai basil and new 1/4 teaspoon fine salt. Original unspecified kosher salt had been deliberately removed; there is no established fine-salt conversion or present need to reinstate it. The original export used 2 tablespoons lime but the later rewrite deliberately reduced it; keep the current tablespoon rather than automatically reverting that choice. Restore the missing quarter teaspoon pepper flakes as optional: the original establishes the quantity, while the current method still calls for them despite their absent ingredient line. This correction does not make extra heat mandatory.

The entire milk can is stirred together. One-quarter disperses paste in a gentle moist simmer, then the rest, sugar, half fish sauce and optional flakes follow. No oil is added and no cream cap or forced coconut splitting is required. Light milk remains deliberately lighter; a full-fat same-volume substitution is clearly richer and still uses the wet method. Pepper/onion cook before thin beef enters, then basil and half the lime go in off heat with remaining fish sauce/lime conditional after tasting. Serving wedges remain separate. Hot water corrects excessive evaporation only; the earlier unmeasured recipe is not silently extended by a mandatory 60 ml sauce addition. Every listed ingredient has a destination.

A deep roughly 3-quart skillet gives room for liquid and solids. Smaller batches need a smaller pan; larger quantities use separate pans/batches to avoid a large cold load overwhelming the broth. Removed precise quarter/half vessel promises that had not been tested. All split quantities use proportions of the scaled ingredient list, not fixed spoons. Beef remains thin, about 3 mm across the grain; chuck cannot replace it in this method. Tenderloin/flank geometry is retained, with flank honestly described as chewier. Use a fine-tip probe inserted from a slice edge to check representative thicker pieces for 145°F, then allow at least three minutes off heat before serving. This is not a claim that every piece takes exactly 2–3 minutes. Cool broth must regain a simmer before timing the finish. Extended reduction occurs before beef, not by boiling the finished slices until tough.

The 45-minute planning total budgets 20 minutes slicing/measuring plus heating, paste development, 6–8-minute vegetable simmer, broth recovery and approximately 2–3-minute beef finish, and the rest. Optional freezer firming adds 20–30 minutes; rice/side recipes have their own clocks. The four small portions retain the source's 12-ounce allocation rather than silently doubling meat to meet a generic rule. Removed the invented 3–4-cup finished yield and 3/4–1-cup servings. Actual satisfaction and volume require cooking.

Do Chua is a small condiment, not the vegetable-side portion; eggplant has its own 45-minute preparation including its sauce. No direct meal references exist for this slug. Massaman also has a long braise, potatoes, whole nuts and tamarind. No duplicate consolidation is warranted.
