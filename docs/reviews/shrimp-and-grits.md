## Grits: product, hydration, finish and yield

The original's absolute twenty-five-minute stone-ground rule is not universally correct, but neither is it universally false. Direct producer guidance matters: Palmetto Farms publishes a four-to-one liquid/grits ratio and twenty-to-twenty-five-minute covered cooking for its grits; Anson Mills coarse grits need roughly fifty minutes after soaking or ninety minutes without. The proposal names Palmetto Farms as the base product and tells cooks to check their bag before planning. It does not silently replace stone-ground grits with quick or instant grits to retain a speed label.

Retained one dry cup grits and four cups cooking water, with a separately listed cup of hot adjustment water used only as needed. The approximate metric water amounts are culinary rounding, not a second different ratio. A fixed gram equivalent was not invented for the named dry cup; record its actual weight in testing. The main now chooses water, as the old method actually used, rather than treating any salted chicken broth as a flavor-neutral alternative. The published producer method uses milk/broth, so this water-only cheesy formulation remains our editorial version of the verified ratio and time range.

Taste a cooled grain before dairy is added. A stiff mass can still have hard centers; add water and continue cooking. The three-quart heavy pot has space for roughly a liter of starting water, grits and later dairy, with enough headroom for stirring. Stir across the bottom, use gentle heat and let particle tenderness govern the endpoint. Do not cite Anson Mills' separate aromatic-oil/boiling explanation as scientific proof; only its product-dependent hydration/time guidance is used.

Kept three tablespoons/42 g butter and two cups/225 g sharp cheddar. This remains a rich cheese-grits bowl, not an unsolicited reduced-fat reformulation. Fold them in off heat once the grain is tender. Cheddar thickens the grits; bounded additional hot water and a short covered warm hold manage texture. There is no invented compulsory long resting interval before serving shrimp. A four-to-five-cup finished grits yield is provisional: water loss, grind and dilution alter volume. Divide among four rather than assuming raw ingredient volumes add up to a precise cooked yield.

A plain quick-grits substitution names a different product and directs its actual package method, with about forty to forty-five minutes for the whole main rather than applying the stone-ground simmer unchanged. The very coarse alternative follows its mill's liquid method and can extend the main to about two hours. The final adaptation specifies the unsoaked coarse-grits method and does not adopt an overnight-soak alternative. Instant flavored packets are not declared equivalent.

## Shrimp, bacon and sauce accounting

Defined 450 g raw shrimp as peeled, deveined and tail-off weight, with a 31–40-per-pound package count instead of the ambiguous medium label. This gives approximately eight to ten shrimp per diner and 113 g raw shrimp per bowl. Shell-on shopping weight must be higher; thawing and extra peeling are outside the base schedule and disclosed. This is a reasonable modest shrimp portion beside substantial cheese grits and bacon, not a claim of a large pure-seafood serving. Actual cooked yield and diner satisfaction need testing.

Bacon is 120 g regular-cut raw weight rather than six potentially very different slices. That is an editorial standardization of the existing ingredient, not a claim that every six slices weigh 120 g. Cook it to a crisp garnish and keep it out of the wet pan coating until plating. Retain exactly one tablespoon rendered fat, split between two 225 g shrimp batches, plus the unavoidable thin pan film. The original kept every drop from its unspecified bacon thickness, which could make an already rich bowl greasy. No extra sauce butter or oil is assumed.

All three tablespoons butter and the cheddar belong to the grits. A newly listed quarter-cup water loosens the shrimp-pan residue and distributes the garlic/scallion/lemon flavor into light pan juices. This is an editorial addition, not an implied full stock gravy or a hidden second sauce. Reduced heat protects the garlic; a teaspoon from the measured water can soften scallion whites in a dry pan before adding the rest. Shrimp come out at their endpoint and return off heat only to coat, avoiding several extra minutes of cooking during sauce work.

The original four teaspoons lemon juice remain the maximum, with three used initially and one held for tasting. The scallion cup becomes an estimated 100 g, divided into whites for cooking and greens for the finish; the garnish comes from this amount instead of unlisted extra scallions. Two tablespoons parsley and one large garlic clove remain. Half a teaspoon black pepper is split between grits and shrimp. Half a teaspoon fine salt is allocated only to the grits, half initially and half after cheddar is tasted. Bacon, cheddar and shrimp's own processing/salinity can supply enough seasoning to the topping; no automatic extra shrimp salt is inserted.

The sauce should lightly coat shrimp and mingle with creamy grits, not form a bacon-fat puddle. Taste a whole bite before final salt/acid. Removed the claims that shrimp must be crispy or that a brief sear guarantees savory crunch; bacon supplies the crisp contrast and shrimp should remain springy. Overcooking cannot be repaired by more butter or sauce. Removed inherited nutrition, whose very low carbohydrate and high protein figures did not support the defined servings or grain quantity; no new calculator figure stands in for measured yield.

## Sequence, time and equipment

Main timing is sixty minutes: twenty minutes preparation, including water heating and ingredient work; twenty to twenty-five minutes grits simmer; roughly fifteen minutes to finish grits, cook two shrimp batches and make the short pan coating. Bacon's eight-to-ten-minute stage overlaps grits. This is an honest planning allowance, not a stopwatch test. Slow grits, a weak burner, peeling or thawing extend it.

The base is fully hydrated and finished before shrimp start. Its brief covered hold around ten minutes is an overlap with seafood cooking, not a separately added rest. A twelve-inch skillet gets two roughly 225 g shrimp rounds to preserve pan contact and allow controlled turning. One overcrowded pound is not assumed to brown in the original two-minute instruction. Thermometer and center appearance replace pink exterior alone: FDA gives the general 145°F seafood endpoint and firm, pearly, opaque shrimp flesh. Several largest pieces are checked with a thin sensing tip; any unusually thick or translucent pieces continue cooking. No arbitrary C-versus-O curl rule is used.

The final raw-to-cooked transfer uses a clean plate. Frozen shrimp are thawed cold, raw seafood is kept refrigerated and used promptly, and cooked components cool separately in shallow containers. Three-to-four-day leftover advice is conservative general guidance rather than a tested shelf-life claim. Reheating to the cooked-leftover endpoint makes shrimp firmer, which is acknowledged; freshly prepared shrimp do not need a meat-style rest while dinner cools.
