This is a correction allowance, not water to add automatically.
3. **Chow fun, step 7:** Before repeating step 6, explicitly let the empty pan recover medium-high heat and heat the next measured oil portion until shimmering before adding aromatics. This distinguishes useful heat recovery from the discarded instruction to maintain smoke regardless of burning.

## Beef and broccoli: passing decisions and limits

The original has 680 g-equivalent steak, five cups broccoli, a substantial soy-oyster-vinegar-sesame sauce, no marinade, an incomplete oil allocation and a claim that a burnt smell proves correct heat. The proposal preserves the main formula and natural steak texture while replacing the unreliable methods. It does not import another author's marinade merely to make its source citations match.

- **Cut and portion:** 680 g trimmed top sirloin means 170 g raw edible beef per diner. Together with 112.5 g trimmed broccoli per diner and separately prepared rice, four substantial main portions are credible. Flank remains a clearly firmer alternative; stew cubes are not presented as interchangeable. The across-grain direction and quarter-inch slice thickness are useful and compatible with the measured cooking endpoint.
- **Sauce:** 60 ml regular soy, 30 ml oyster sauce, 15 ml vinegar and 120 ml unsalted stock/water provide approximately 225 ml of water-based ingredients before sesame oil and meat juices. Two teaspoons cornstarch are credible for a light glaze, accounting for oyster sauce's own body. The original tablespoon sesame oil is deliberately retained. This is a savory sauce for plain rice; avoid reducing it until sticky. Brand-dependent saltiness and sesame intensity remain taste-test questions, not grounds to arbitrarily dilute or reduce the original flavoring.
- **Oil:** Two broccoli portions use 3 tsp combined; three beef batches use 4.5 tsp; aromatics use 1.5 tsp. Total 9 tsp equals the listed 3 tbsp. Sesame oil is separately accounted for and enters after thickening.
- **Capacity and order:** Two 225 g broccoli batches and three approximately 225 g beef batches are credible household portions. Cooking broccoli before beef avoids a lengthy hold for the steak. Two final combinations avoid putting all 1.1 kg of raw-weight components plus sauce back into one skillet for a sear. Their purpose is coating and reheating. Stem tenderness, sauce flow and a thermometer check each address a different endpoint.
- **Timing:** Roughly 25 minutes preparation plus 30 minutes heat/batch work is credible, with initial heating, 10–12 minutes broccoli, 6–12 minutes beef, heat recovery, sauce and two final folds. All upper ends together can approach an hour. The revised wording acknowledging slower batches is appropriate. The final sauce and combination work naturally supplies the three-minute beef rest; it is also explicitly checked before serving.
- **Relationship:** The current reviewed plain short-grain rice takes 65 minutes from dry rice. The draft correctly calls rice separate and includes broccoli in the main; its 55 minutes is not a promise for the whole rice dinner. No referencing meal or recipe was found in the content search. Do not add an automatic second broccoli side.

## Beef chow fun: passing decisions and limits

The saved HTML explicitly credits The Woks of Life and specifies 225 g beef, 340 g fresh noodles and two servings. The later repository version increased the beef to about 450 g and the serving count to four, but only used about 450 g noodles and omitted all cooking oil from its ingredient list. Restoring 450 g beef with 680 g fresh noodles preserves the archived protein-to-noodle ratio. It is a material and justified correction, not merely changing bowl count.

- **Ingredient identity:** Flank, broad rice noodles, ginger, scallions, sprouts and a thin soy coating distinguish this from the broccoli dish's vegetables and gravy. Keep both recipes. The source credit is established by the archive; it is not inferred from a similar current recipe.
- **Other actual variants:** The current Mongolian beef has a 24 g dry starch coating and 40 g brown sugar, with ginger/scallions and a separate slurry. Chicken-and-broccoli has a wine/starch chicken coating and a different stock/oyster sauce. Pad see ew has chicken, egg, gai lan and Thai sweet dark soy. These composition and technique differences support retaining the assigned dishes; shared beef, broccoli or broad noodles alone do not make them duplicates.
- **Tenderization:** A quarter teaspoon baking soda per 450 g falls within the source's small-dose no-rinse guidance. This differs from the much larger treatment that is rinsed away. The twenty-minute rest is an adaptation of the current recipe, not an exact published test of this whole mixture. The recipe limits treatment and explains pH without claiming that oil seals in juices. Two teaspoons starch and a separate teaspoon oil make the coating explicit. Loose raw-contact residue is not poured into finished noodles.
- **Noodle state and portions:** The baseline is 680 g fully cooked fresh noodles, divided into four 170 g portions. The package-specific warming and gentle separation instructions solve the cold block problem without a universal microwave promise. Dried 250 g means 62.5 g dry noodles per diner: a practical moderate alternative with beef, not a claim that 250 g must hydrate to 680 g. The varying hydrated yield, package preparation and extra time are disclosed. No finished cooked weight is claimed.
- **Seasoning and material changes:** The proposed noodle quantity returns to the archived ratio while the modern recipe's regular soy, dark soy, sugar and sesame quantities largely remain. Added noodle wine, coating water and coating starch are deliberate changes. The archive's cooking oil would be 6 tbsp if the entire formula were doubled; the proposal uses 4 tbsp plus 1 tsp coating oil. This must remain described as a household-pan adaptation, not an exact doubling of every source ingredient. On paper the soy coating is plausible for about 1.3 kg ingredients; actual brand, noodle oiliness, seasoning coverage and fat perception need a kitchen test.
- **Oil:** 1 tsp for sprouts + 3 tsp for the two beef batches + 8 tsp for four noodle batches equals 12 tsp / 4 tbsp cooking oil. The coating teaspoon is additional. Each noodle batch receives 2 tsp, so the extra portions are not cooked in unspecified residual oil.
- **Capacity and order:** Each finishing batch contains 170 g noodles, one quarter of the cooked beef from 112.5 g raw, about 30 g raw-weight sprouts, scallions and seasoning. This is a conservative household-pan load. Sprouts cook separately to steaming-hot, softened stems; they are not treated as safe merely because a thirty-second toss wilts them. Four finishing rounds preserve individual ribbons but mean progressive service. The draft candidly permits serving as each portion finishes and warns that waiting plates cool and soften; it does not promise all four freshly fried plates at once.
- **Timing:** About 30 minutes preparation can contain slicing followed by the twenty-minute refrigerated rest and parallel noodle/vegetable work. Another 35 minutes allows initial heat, roughly 3–5 minutes sprouts, 4–8 minutes beef, transfers/recovery and 12–16 minutes noodle finishing. The 65-minute total is reasonable, with cold-noodle variation and optional freezer firming outside the guarantee. Preparing an optional side is additional work.
- **Relationships:** The actual reviewed steamed broccoli is a 15-minute, four-small-portion vegetable side. The actual garlic-sesame-spinach slug is Korean sigeumchi namul: 20 minutes and four small banchan portions. Either can accompany noodles by flavor and texture; neither should be described as a required component or misidentified as Chinese stir-fried spinach. Extra rice would duplicate the main's starch. No meal references were found.

## Kitchen-test questions, not editorial assertions

Measure the actual trimmed steak and noodle brands, salt levels, cooked yield, pan/burner, oil remaining in the pan, active versus elapsed time, and first versus last plate quality. Does the quarter-inch sirloin retain a pleasing bite after searing to the stated endpoint and two finishing rounds? Does the broccoli glaze coat without seeming salty or sesame-heavy? For chow fun, how long does the chosen chilled noodle block need, does 2 tsp oil per finishing portion prevent sticking without greasiness, does the twenty-minute coating taste clean, and are four moderate portions satisfying with the intended side? Compare the dried-noodle option separately rather than certifying it from a dry-to-fresh arithmetic assumption.
