# Brisket with Carrots and Onions: individual editorial review

Date: 2026-09-06. Editorial review only; no physical kitchen test, measured
yield, telemetry, or reproducibility claim.

The review used the current source, its local history, the Group A and braises
pilot records, the recipe standard, and FoodSafety.gov/Cattlemen’s Beef Board
sources. No external author or family attribution appears in the source history.
The Jewish holiday context remains in the cuisine metadata, while unsupported
universal historical claims were removed.

The finished proposal preserves the defining formula: a 6–7 pound flat-cut
brisket; 2 tablespoons kosher salt, 2 teaspoons pepper, 1 tablespoon garlic and
2 teaspoons oregano; 1 pound carrots, 8 celery stalks, 6 onions, 6 bay leaves;
and the full 46-ounce can of tomato juice. It remains a no-sear tomato braise,
with vegetables and against-the-grain slicing. No nutrition fields are carried
forward because the legacy values are not credible for the stated meat and
vegetable formula and were not recalculated.

Every ingredient has an explicit destination: seasoning rub; vegetables and bay
leaves in the pan; all tomato juice in the covered braise; bay leaves discarded
before service; meat, vegetables and reduced juice served together. The existing
manufacturer-approval/transfer condition for the pan and bay-leaf removal are
preserved. The conflicting instruction that the fixed can must reach a particular
depth was replaced with a pan-capacity rule: use the full can, choose a pan with
headspace, and do not add or omit juice to reach a visual mark.
If the sauce reduces too far, it can be loosened with hot water in tablespoon
additions; remove catching sauce from heat and transfer only unburned sauce without scraping. Burned bitterness is not recoverable with water. Dry braising liquid can also be replenished with a little hot water before continuing covered cooking.

Timing is a planning estimate only: roughly 20 minutes to heat and assemble, 3
1/2 hours covered at 350°F / 175°C, and 20–30 minutes reduction while the meat
rests. A thick brisket may need 15-minute covered extensions; the reduction waits
until fork tenderness. A 145°F / 63°C, three-minute-rest safety check was added
for the whole beef roast, with fork tenderness kept as the separate quality
endpoint. Storage now specifies shallow containers, the 2-hour/1-hour cooling
limit, 40°F / 4°C refrigeration, a 3–4 day window, freezing later portions, and
165°F / 74°C reheating.

Sources: [Cattlemen’s Beef Board braising basics](https://www.beefitswhatsfordinner.com/cooking/braising-basics)
supports a covered beef braise and fork-tender endpoint; [FoodSafety.gov cooling](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/4-steps-to-food-safety),
[cold storage](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts),
and [safe temperatures](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures)
support the safety guidance. None verifies the original formula, timing, pan
geometry, or yield.

It is not a duplicate of this tomato-juice, six-onion recipe and is not changed or counted as reviewed here. No meal directly references this tomato-juice brisket.

Kitchen tests still needed: exact brisket thickness and 6–7 pound pan fit; tomato
juice depth and sealing in a real pan; time to tenderness; sauce reduction and
salt balance; ten-serving yield; reheating; and half/double-batch vessel capacity.

Integration check: fixed scaling is explicit for the whole 6–7 pound flat and roasting-pan geometry. This prevents ingredient quantities from multiplying while the method still calls for one 46-ounce can. Separate full pans are the larger-service option. The wide-saucepan substitution now acknowledges evaporation-rate differences rather than promising identical reduction time. All original ingredient quantities are preserved.
