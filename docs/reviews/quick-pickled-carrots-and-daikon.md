## Individual ratio and accounting decisions

**150 g peeled/trimmed carrot + 150 g peeled/trimmed daikon** preserves equal amounts while eliminating differences between loosely filled julienne cups, densely packed shredded cups and whole-root purchase sizes. The 300 g total is a chosen amount intended to give approximately two cups **drained finished pickles**, not a claim that the old two cups raw vegetables weighed exactly 300 g. Some liquid is lost during massaging, and packing/thickness change final volume. Expected drained mass may be around 230–270 g, but that is an estimate requiring measurement; the recipe does not advertise an exact finished weight.

Use **1/2 tsp fine sea salt**, roughly 3 g or 1% of initial vegetable mass, instead of an ambiguous teaspoon of kosher salt. Add **1 tsp sugar** for this softening step. Both are largely rinsed away with the released water; actual retained amounts are unknown. A three-minute gentle knead should produce bendable daikon and visible liquid, not necessarily limp carrot. The 2 mm carrot and 2–3 mm daikon cuts make that endpoint realistic; stiff sticks may need a few extra minutes. “Bone dry” forceful wringing is removed because it damages the strips and is unnecessary. The massaging/rinsing step is for texture and flavor, not proof of sterilization or long-term preservation.

Keep the full existing **1/4 cup granulated sugar**, approximately 50 g, **in the brine**, with **1/2 cup unseasoned rice vinegar + 1/2 cup water**, about 120 ml each. The separately added massage teaspoon means total sugar measured into the recipe is slightly higher than the old ingredient list, but most of that extra teaspoon is discarded; this is not a concealed sugar cut or an assertion all 54 g is eaten. The 50 g/240 ml initial liquid is a plainly sweet-tart brine, broadly comparable in concentration to Nguyen's sweet brine though it uses a different vegetable/vinegar ratio. Pickles are drained for serving, so ingredient arithmetic cannot supply credible per-serving nutrition without uptake/yield measurements. Legacy nutrition is removed.

Unseasoned rice vinegar preserves current flavor. A label of **4–5% acidity** explicitly allows common 4.3% rice vinegar and avoids falsely requiring all rice vinegar to be 5%. Distilled white 5% is a same-volume alternative with a sharper taste. The defined vinegar/water ratio is not a pH test, and the brine is not asserted to be shelf-stable. Seasoned rice vinegar contributes unknown extra sugar/salt and is not substituted blindly. The draft includes no fish sauce, soy, garlic, chile or sesame; adding them would alter this simple sweet-sour condiment's role alongside already seasoned mains.

The dissolved sugar raises brine volume somewhat above its initial 240 ml, to roughly 270 ml. With the softened vegetable solids, the total can approach a tightly packed pint's usable volume; a **750 ml jar** provides practical room for packing/headspace. The vegetables must remain below liquid. A wide jar or coarse loose sticks may need a narrower vessel or additional same-ratio brine. The optional correction is explicitly listed: two tablespoons vinegar, two tablespoons water and one tablespoon sugar, used only as needed for coverage. Topping up with plain water would alter the chosen flavor/acidity. Coverage is judged on the actual jar rather than promised from a raw-cup count. A clean jar is sufficient for this refrigerator method; no canning sterilization process is invented.

## Timing, temperature and storage

Choose **80 minutes elapsed**: approximately twenty minutes for washing/peeling/weighing, knife julienne, three-minute massage, rinse/drain, brine mixing and packing, then **one hour in the refrigerator**. Fifteen-minute prep might work with a practiced cook or cutting tool, but the slower knife-work allowance is more defensible. Smaller batches reduce cutting, not the one-hour pickling interval. First-use pickles are sweet-tart and crisp; overnight refrigeration develops flavor further. Their color is not a maturation or safety test. No stovetop or burner is required: dissolve sugar in lukewarm water then add vinegar, with no hot brine on the delicate strips.

A [WSU Clark County Master Food Preservers Do Chua handout](https://s3.wp.wsu.edu/uploads/sites/2079/2016/05/Do-Chua.pdf) uses equal carrot/daikon weights and rice vinegar, but a different 1:3 vinegar/water ratio, more salt, no rinse and a longer twenty-four-hour flavor wait. It specifically avoids a hot pour that may soften daikon. It is corroborating refrigerator technique and variation, **not the tested source of this new formula or its exact one-week storage**. [OSU Extension refrigerator vegetables](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/imported-publication/refrigerator-pickled-vegetables) supports clean jars, close packing, brine coverage and refrigeration, with its own different recipe and duration.

Retain the existing **one-week refrigerator allowance** at 40°F/4°C or below as an editorial limit for this version; do not import Nguyen's four weeks, WSU's three weeks or OSU's three months as though these formulas are interchangeable. Keep submerged, use clean utensils, do not add new vegetables to used brine, and discard spoiled contents. This remains a fresh refrigerator condiment, not a validated canning recipe, fermentation, pantry preserve, challenge-tested shelf life or measured-pH process. The sensory note does not repeat “stinky means safe”; daikon has a recognizable aroma, but odor alone cannot establish safe storage.
